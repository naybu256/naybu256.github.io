## The takeaway

AGD anti-principles challenge clones, volume without diversity, certainty without data and averages without context. These are editorial and operational choices.

## The argument

The problem with miracle recipes is not only that they may be wrong. They often prevent the useful questions. Why this content for this audience? What does this learning cost? What happens if the result is negative? A practice that never allows for failure is rarely an experiment.

When advice arrives with a spectacular result, ask about attempts missing from the screenshot. The advice may still be interesting even if its author measured little. Its status changes: a lead to explore, not a rule to impose. This distinction avoids replacing one dogma with another. Skepticism is useful when it clarifies the next test, not when it automatically dismisses every observation.

## Write down the tempting shortcuts while they are still hypothetical.

It is easier to reject fabricated proof before a campaign misses its target. Put the red lines where the pressure appears: no invented customer identities in a testimonial, no unsupported causal claim in a report, no republished creator asset without an appropriate basis, and no account identity whose usefulness depends on misleading the audience. These are specific editorial decisions. They should not dissolve into a vague promise to be authentic.

Then consider the operational incentives. If a team rewards only volume, people will optimise for volume. If every unsuccessful test is punished, somebody will eventually redefine success. A useful review includes the result the team hoped for, the result it observed and what remains unknown. Keep the option to stop a weak idea without dressing the stop as a secret win. A publication with a pirate tone can challenge lazy marketing conventions without making its evidence less trustworthy. The edge should live in the point of view, not in the accuracy of the footnotes.
