## The argument

A good opening makes a promise the rest fulfills. “I lost my notes before the exam” creates a specific situation. “This app will change your life” could sell anything. Compare the first sentence with the conclusion: if they address different problems, early retention may measure delayed disappointment.

Try rewriting without commands or spectacular adjectives. Replace the demand to stay with the situation you will resolve. If the opening becomes clearer, precision has improved. Then evaluate the full piece: a quieter hook may attract fewer curious viewers and more people facing the actual problem. That possibility is worth testing, not asserting as a universal advantage of restrained creative work.

## Replace the command with something worth noticing.

A hook that tells everyone to stop scrolling spends its first moment talking about the feed rather than the viewer’s problem. Try opening on the problem itself. A meeting note with no decision, a recipe that fails at one visible step, or an object doing something unexpected gives the audience a concrete reason to pay attention. The useful question is not whether the opening sounds energetic. It is whether the intended viewer can recognise the promised reward.

Write three openings for the same payoff: one based on a visible problem, one on a contradiction and one on an unfinished action. Keep the ending fixed so the team can discuss the relationship between promise and delivery. Do not judge an opening in isolation from the rest of the piece. A spectacular first frame that attracts the wrong expectation can damage comprehension later. When reviewing results, keep the actual creative version next to the metric. Otherwise a successful concept becomes a vague instruction to use stronger hooks, and the next production batch returns to the same empty command.

The garden-furniture example starts from a specific, understandable problem described in the source: dust-covered furniture after rain. That context gives the viewer a reason to anticipate a resolution without a generic claim that everything they know is wrong. For an original post, make a list of the details that would let someone recognise the problem instantly. Choose the most legible one for the opening and preserve the rest for the explanation. A strong hook can be a clear observation. It does not always need a threat, a secret or a sentence written as though the entire internet has been waiting for this tip.

### Three alternatives to shouting

- **Problem:** Show the frustrating moment the viewer recognises.

- **Contradiction:** Put two apparently incompatible facts in the same frame.

- **Open action:** Begin something whose outcome the viewer wants to see.
